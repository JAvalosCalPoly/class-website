Today we will discuss kernels of group homomorphisms, which are the group theoretic analog of kernels (null spaces) of the linear transformations you saw in linear algebra.

Kernels of homomorphisms

Often we care about precisely how a homomorphism fails to be injective, which we capture with the idea of a kernel.

Given a group homomorphism \(\phi\colon G \to H\), we say that the kernel of the homomorphism is the subset of elements of \(G\) that map to the identity in \(H\): \[ \ker(\phi) = \left\{ g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H \right\}. \]

In the first example (embedding \(D_4\) into \(S_4\)), the kernel is trivial: \[\ker(\phi) = \{e\} \subseteq D_4.\] In the above proposition/proof about the trivial homomorphism, the kernel is all of the domain: \[ \ker(\phi) = G. \] The kernel can be between these two extremes too. There is a homomorphism \(\phi\colon D_6 \to D_3\) which satisfies \(\phi(r_6) = r_3\) and \(\phi(f_6)=f_3\). It sends \(\phi(r_6^3) = r_3^3 = e\), and its kernel is \[ \ker(\phi) = \{e, r_6^3\}. \]

Prove that for every group homomorphism \(\phi\colon G \to H\), the kernel \[\ker(\phi) = \left\{ g \in G \mid \phi(g) = e_H \right\}\] is a normal subgroup of \(G\).
(Hint: first prove \(\ker(\phi) \leq G\), and then prove \(\ker(\phi) \triangleleft G\).)

First, we check that the kernel is a subgroup.

Closure
If \(g_1, g_2 \in \ker(\phi)\), then \[ \phi(g_1 * g_2) \in \phi(g_1) \bullet \phi(g_2) = e_H \bullet e_H = e_H, \] and so \(g_1 * g_2 \in \ker(\phi)\).
Associativity
Associativity is inherited from \(G\).
Identity
We've showed previously that homomorphisms map the identity to the identity: \[ \phi(e_G) = e_H. \] Thus \(e_G \in \ker(\phi)\).
Inverses
Similarly, we showed previously that \( \phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g)^{-1}, \), so if \(g \in \ker(\phi)\), then \[ \phi(g^{-1}) = \phi(g)^{-1} = e_H^{-1} = e_H. \] Thus \(g^{-1} \in \ker(\phi)\).

Therefore, for every homomorphism \(\phi\), \(\ker(\phi)\) is a subgroup of \(G\).

Lastly, we check that the kernel is a normal subgroup. Suppose that \(g \in G\) and \(n \in \ker(\phi)\). Then \[ \phi(g * n * g^{-1}) = \phi(g) \bullet \underbrace{\phi(n)}_{e_H} \bullet \underbrace{\phi(g^{-1})}_{\phi(g)^{-1}} = \phi(g) \bullet \phi(g)^{-1} = e_H, \] and so for all \(g \in G\) and \(n \in \ker(\phi)\), \(g * n * g^{-1} \in \ker(\phi)\), and thus the kernel is a normal subgroup.

Remember why we care about normal subgroups: because we can quotient by them! Soon we will learn about the first isomorphism theorem which tells us precisely what happens when we quotient a group \(G\) by the kernel of a homomorphism. (Hint: it relates to the image of the homomorphism.)
